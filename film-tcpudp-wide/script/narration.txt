# CHAPTER 1 The Best-Effort Wire
You're on a video call with a friend,|and mid-sentence, you send a photo.
Your voice and your photo|leave the same machine,|over the same wire.
They will not travel the same way.

Underneath every app,|the internet has one delivery service:|packets — small envelopes of data.
Each envelope carries about 1,500 bytes,|addressed to your friend's machine.
Your photo is three megabytes —|more than two thousand envelopes.

That delivery service is called IP,|the Internet Protocol.
And here is the strange part:|IP promises you almost nothing.
No acknowledgments. No retransmissions.|No ordering. No flow control.

That is not a flaw.|It's the design.
Keep the network simple;|put the intelligence at the endpoints.

So in transit, an envelope can be lost,|delivered out of order,|or copied twice.
For your photo, that would be fatal.
For your voice, it might not matter.

Two kinds of traffic, one careless wire.
The internet's answer was never|one perfect protocol.
It was two —|with opposite promises.

# CHAPTER 2 TCP — The Courier
The first is TCP,|from a 1974 paper by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn,|sealed as RFC 793 in 1981.

Before TCP moves any data, it makes a call.
SYN — "Can you hear me?"
SYN-ACK — "I hear you. Can you hear me?"
ACK — "I hear you."
Three messages, and both sides agree|on where the numbering starts.
## gap 12

Then the rules of the courier.
Every envelope is numbered.|Every number is acknowledged.
If a receipt doesn't come back in time,|the letter is sent again —|and the wait doubles:|one second, two, then four.

On the other end,|the receiver re-stacks the envelopes in order,|and holds the gap open|until the missing one arrives.

The receiver also advertises|how much it can hold — a window —|so a fast sender|never drowns a slow one.
## gap 12

And TCP listens to the network.
When envelopes start disappearing,|it cuts its sending rate in half;|when they flow again,|it ramps carefully back up.

That's the contract:|what you send arrives —|complete and in order —|or the connection tells you it failed.
The price: talk before you talk,|remember everything,|and pay at least twenty bytes|of instructions on every envelope.

Your photo rides TCP,|and it arrives perfect.
But your voice can't dial before every word.|Fifty times a second,|it has something to say.
Sometimes, waiting is worse than losing.

# CHAPTER 3 UDP — The Postcard
For that, there is a second protocol:|UDP, from RFC 768, 1980, by Jon Postel.

A UDP packet is a postcard.
You write it, you address it,|you drop it in the mail.|That's the whole ceremony.
No handshake. No numbers. No receipts.|Eight bytes of header, four small fields.

The spec is honest:|delivery is not guaranteed.
Even duplicate protection|is not guaranteed.
## gap 15

And still, the internet runs on it.

Because sometimes the exchange is smaller|than the ceremony would be.
A DNS lookup is one small question|and one small answer —|usually one packet each way.
A handshake would cost more|than the delivery.
## gap 15

And sometimes, late means dead.
Your voice is a stream of packets,|one every twenty milliseconds.
If one is lost, the call doesn't wait|a second for the copy to arrive.
The moment is already gone.
It plays a tiny gap, and moves on.

A multiplayer game makes the same choice:|your position right now|matters more than|your position a second ago.
## gap 12

UDP isn't careless.
It's honest about what the wire already is —|and it lets the application decide|what is worth waiting for.

# CHAPTER 4 Choosing Sides
So the rule of thumb.
Need every byte?|TCP: web pages, files, email, banking.
Need it live?|UDP: calls, games, lookups.

Both protocols share one trick: ports —|sixteen-bit numbers,|zero to sixty-five thousand|five hundred thirty-five.
A space for TCP,|and a separate space for UDP.
Port 443 speaks TCP.|Port 53 speaks UDP.

And the story has one more twist.
The newest version of HTTP —|HTTP/3, standardized in 2022 —|no longer rides TCP. It rides QUIC.
And QUIC, standardized in 2021,|is built on UDP.

Handshakes, sequence numbers,|recovery, encryption —|everything TCP does,|rebuilt above the wire,|where apps can evolve.
A QUIC connection carries an ID,|not an address —|switch from Wi-Fi to cellular,|and it survives.
## gap 15

So the real question|was never "reliable or fast".
It's where the reliability lives:|frozen in every kernel,|or in the application,|one update away.
## gap 15

Back to your call.
Your voice rides UDP —|unnumbered, unwaited, alive.
Your photo rides TCP —|numbered, receipted, perfect.
Same wire. Same envelopes.|Two promises.
## gap 15

TCP never loses.
UDP never waits.
