# PCAP — Packet Capture, Explained — narration v1 (| splits subtitles only; EN block <=48 chars)
# CHAPTER 1 The Mystery
Your checkout page takes nine seconds to load.|Customers give up on it.
The server logs say|every request was answered|in about sixty milliseconds.
Both readings are true.|The missing time lives somewhere between them.

To find it, you have to see|what the network actually carried.
Everything you send travels as packets —|small envelopes of data.
Each one has a header,|the address on the envelope,|and a payload, the letter inside.
And they nest: TCP rides inside IP,|IP rides inside Ethernet.
One packet carries about 1,500 bytes —|roughly one paragraph of text.

A packet capture is exactly what it sounds like:|catching a copy of the packets|as they pass a point in the network.
Not intercepting. Not changing.|Just copying —|a checkpoint that photocopies|every envelope that drives past.
With that copy in hand,|you can replay the whole conversation:|every byte, every timestamp.
It's how networks are debugged,|how intrusions are caught,|and how our nine seconds get explained.

Engineers have done this for decades.|tcpdump was born at Berkeley Lab in 1988.
Ten years later, Gerald Combs|built a graphical analyzer around it —|Ethereal, renamed Wireshark in 2006.
It's what most of the internet|debugs its networks with.

# CHAPTER 2 How It Works
The tool is the easy part.|The real question is where you stand|to get your copy of the packets.|Three answers.
On your own machine, the operating system|already sees its own traffic —|that's the easy one.
On a shared switch,|port mirroring copies one port's traffic|to a monitoring port.
And on the physical link,|a network tap passively splits the signal|and hands you a copy.
## gap 15

On your own machine, one catch:|a network card normally drops|frames addressed to someone else.
Promiscuous mode tells it:|keep everything you see.|Wireshark turns this on by default.

Between the hardware and the tool|sits a small, old, brilliant stack:|libpcap on Linux and macOS,|Npcap on Windows.
And underneath, a kernel filter from 1992:|the Berkeley Packet Filter.
It drops the packets you don't want|before they ever leave the kernel,|so filtering a firehose stays cheap.

Whatever survives the filter|lands in a capture file.|The classic format is called pcap.
Per packet, it stores exactly three things:|a timestamp, a length,|and the raw bytes.
Its successor, pcapng,|is what modern Wireshark saves by default.
Nothing is decoded yet.|Interpretation happens later,|on your machine, every time you open it.
Which makes the file evidence, not testimony.|It never argues.|It just remembers.

# CHAPTER 3 Wireshark
Open that file in Wireshark,|and you get three zoom levels|on the same evidence.
The packet list: one row per packet,|time, source, destination, protocol.
The packet details:|the envelope peeled open layer by layer,|Ethernet, IP, TCP, TLS.
The packet bytes: raw hexadecimal,|exactly as it crossed the wire.

Too many packets?|Two different filters.
A capture filter runs while recording:|kernel-level, strict syntax, cheap.
A display filter runs after the fact:|http or dns,|just hiding what you don't need.
And when you find one conversation,|Follow TCP Stream|reassembles it into a readable transcript.

So — the checkout page.|Filter to the API host,|follow the stream,|and read the timestamps.
The request leaves instantly.|The response doesn't arrive.
The same data is sent again, and again —|each gap doubling:|one second, two, then four.
Those are retransmissions.|A letter vanished in transit,|and the sender waited by the mailbox|on a doubling timer.
Wireshark even marks them for you,|in its expert information.
Seven seconds of waiting,|inside a response|the server logged as sixty milliseconds.
Packet loss on the path.|The server never knew.|The browser couldn't say why.|Only the wire saw it.

# CHAPTER 4 Limits & Laws
Before you go capturing everything,|two honest limits.
First, encryption.|Almost everything now rides TLS 1.3,|whose handshake takes a single round trip,|and whose content you cannot read.
You still see the envelopes:|who talked to whom, when, and how much.
That was enough for our mystery —|we never read the checkout data,|only its timing.
## gap 20

Second, permission.|Capturing traffic you're not authorized to see|is a crime in many places,|and a firing offense almost anywhere.
So get explicit permission first.
On networks you own, capture everything.|It's the best way to learn this craft.

As for our checkout page:|the loss traced to a failing switch port,|and nine seconds became under one.
The lesson outlives the story.
A log tells you|what a program believed happened.
A capture tells you|what actually happened.
The wire doesn't lie.
