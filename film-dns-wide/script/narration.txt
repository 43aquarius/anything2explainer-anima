# DNS — The Internet's Phonebook — narration v1 (| splits subtitles only; EN block <=48 chars)
# CHAPTER 1 The Mystery
You type shop.example and press Enter.|Half a second later, the page appears.
Here is the strange part:|no machine on the internet|is named shop.example.
The internet doesn't speak names.|It speaks numbers.
Names are for humans.|Routing is for machines.
## gap 15

So in the first millisecond,|your browser is holding|an envelope with no address on it.
Before anything can be sent,|the name has to become a number.
Somebody has to run that translation —|every single time you press Enter.

It didn't always work this way.
For the internet's first decade,|there was one text file, HOSTS.TXT —|the whole network's contact list,
maintained by one office at SRI,|updated when you phoned in a change.
Thousands of hosts, one file,|one office, one phone line.|That was the ceiling.
## gap 20

In 1983, Paul Mockapetris|proposed a different design:|no central book at all.
A hierarchy, distributed worldwide —|the Domain Name System.
The spec we still run today|was written in November 1987.|It predates the web itself.
So how do you look things up|in a book that has no center?
## gap 15

# CHAPTER 2 The Lookup
Your browser doesn't do the walking.|It hands the question to a resolver —|yours, or a public one like 1.1.1.1.
Think of the resolver as a librarian|you've hired to fetch one book.
The librarian starts at the top:|the root servers.
The root doesn't know shop.example.|It never will.
It answers one smaller question:|who handles names ending in .example?

That's a referral —|not the answer,|just the address of the next counter.
The next counter is the TLD server|for .example.
Same move: it points to the shop's|authoritative name server.
That server, finally, holds the record:|shop.example — 192.0.2.33.
The answer travels back to your browser.|The envelope gets its address.
Four counters, and none of them|knew the whole book.
The whole walk usually finishes|in well under a tenth of a second.
## gap 15

But if every Enter paid that full price,|the root would melt.
Most of the time, nobody is actually|walking the hallway at all.
## gap 20

# CHAPTER 3 The Cache
Every answer along the way|gets written on a sticky note.
Your browser keeps notes.|Your operating system keeps notes.|Your resolver keeps the biggest stack.
Next time you ask,|the note answers in about a millisecond —|faster than a camera shutter.
Each note carries a TTL —|time to live —|set by the record's owner.
It's the expiry date on the carton.|Fresh until then.|Questioned after.
## gap 15

That's what people call|"DNS propagation" — but nothing is pushed.
You change the record,|and old answers simply sit in caches|until their dates pass.
In 2021, Facebook's nameservers|went dark mid-maintenance.
For about eleven minutes,|the world's caches still served|the last correct copies.
Then they expired —|and facebook.com simply|stopped being an answer anywhere.
That is how much of this system|runs on sticky notes:|1.1.1.1 alone answers|about 1.9 trillion lookups a day.
## gap 15

A phonebook this size, this fast,|this uncritical —|what could possibly go wrong?

# CHAPTER 4 When It Breaks
Three honest answers.
First: the questions themselves|were open postcards for 33 years.
Classic DNS rides plaintext UDP.|Anyone on the path could read|every name you looked up.
The fixes came in 2016 and 2018:|DNS over TLS,|DNS over HTTPS.
Second: the book can lie.
For 19 days in 2003,|every mistyped .com name on Earth|landed on one company's ad page.
No such domain — NXDOMAIN —|is a signal the internet depends on.|That wildcard broke it.
## gap 25


So the answers got signatures:|DNSSEC — a chain of trust|from the root down.
The root itself was signed in 2010,|27 years after DNS was born.
Third: attack the book,|and the readers go down with it.
In 2016, a botnet of hijacked|cameras, printers and baby monitors|flooded one DNS provider.
Twitter, Netflix, Spotify — gone.|Their own servers never crashed.|Only the shared phonebook did.
## gap 20

So — you pressed Enter,|half a second ago.
One question, four counters,|a stack of sticky notes,|one envelope addressed.
A name is a question.|DNS is the answer.
